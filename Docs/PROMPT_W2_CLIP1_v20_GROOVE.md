ACTION
The attached track is the sole timing clock; the pulse is 128 beats per minute.

Underneath the whole shot Paola keeps one continuous groove: her knees bounce softly on every beat and
her weight rocks from foot to foot, her spine loose enough that the bounce travels up through her body
and her head rides it. The groove never stops and never resets — only its size changes with the music.

Gravity completes her drop immediately. Both bare feet strike the stone on the landing bass peak; she
lands wide and low, ankles and knees and hips absorbing the weight, and the rebound carries her straight
into the groove with no pause between the landing and the dance. Her arms arrive in an asymmetrical
Indian shape, one hand high on a diagonal and the other folded low.

While the strings play alone the groove stays small and her feet stay planted, and her arms do the
dancing: her wrists circle continuously, fingers open and articulate through Indian hand shapes, one arm
carving a slow diagonal across her body while the other holds its line. Each strum sharpens an accent
through her wrists. She moves deliberately less than everyone around her. The dancers are busier than
she is — small travelling steps, hip pulses, skirt sway and wrist flourishes — all of them landing the
same beat with their own slight differences in size and timing.

When the heavy drums strike, she drops deeper into her stance and throws one sharp combination: her
ribcage pops, her shoulder answers, her arm snaps out to a hard Indian hand shape, and she freezes
there and holds it while the street keeps moving. At that same strike the dancers explode outward,
turning and crossing and flaring their skirts so the street seems to circle her stillness.

When the drums fall away and the strings return, the freeze releases back into the groove, bigger now
and beginning to travel, her wrists circling again and her other arm opening into a new line. The
phrase is still running when the clip cuts.
